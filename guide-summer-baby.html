Summer Baby Survival Guide: Keeping A Newborn Cool When It's 95 Degrees Outside

Our son was born August 3. In Texas. Here's how we kept a newborn safe when the heat index hit 105.

Full disclosure: this post contains affiliate links; if you buy through them we may earn a small commission at no extra cost. We only recommend what we genuinely used.

Our son was born August 3. In Texas. I know what you're thinking — we thought it too. Newborns can't regulate their body temperature worth a damn, and our pediatrician said the words "heat exhaustion" more than I'd heard them in my entire life. The first outing was a 90-second walk from the parking lot to the clinic, and his onesie was damp by the time we got inside — 96 degrees at 10 a.m. Here's the gear that got us through, and the rules that kept us safe.

The stroller fan — the absolute MVP

The Jisulife clip-on stroller fan was the first thing we bought and the thing we used most. It clamps to the stroller canopy, the car seat handle, even the bassinet edge on still nights. The blades are soft, the battery lasts a full day of errands, and our son was obsessed with it — like it was his favorite television. One honest warning: never point it directly at his face — it's a gentle breeze, not a wind tunnel. Ours was $18 and survived from May to October.

The sun hat — the war we almost lost

Under six months, babies shouldn't wear sunscreen, so the hat does the heavy lifting. The i play flap hat has UPF 50+, a brim that covers ears and neck, and a chin strap that actually stays on — because the "hat on, hat off, hat on the ground" game is the national sport of babies. We went through three: one at the zoo, one at a friend's pool, one still going. For $12 each, the cheapest casualties of the summer.

The sunscreen — mineral only, and patch test first

No chemical sunscreen before six months, per our pediatrician, and even the mineral kind only on small areas. The Babyganics SPF 50 mineral lotion was her pick — zinc oxide, fragrance-free, water-resistant. The first time we applied it, he got a tiny pink patch on his thigh — a sensitivity to one of the plant oils, not a burn; the sheer blend version fixed it. Patch test on the arm, wait a day, then trust the bottle. Apply it like you're frosting a cake — the white cast means the zinc is working.

The cooling towel — for me, until it was for the stroller

I bought the FROGG TOGGS Chilly Pad for myself, because pushing a stroller in 97 degrees is an olympic sport. Then I learned the trick: wet it, wring it, snap it, and drape it over the stroller canopy — never on the baby — and the evaporation cools the air underneath by several degrees. It stays cold for over an hour and lived on my neck during the 4 p.m. Walgreens run.

The portable blackout shade — car naps, restored

Our nursery faces west, and at 6 p.m. it was a sauna with a view. The RUseeN portable blackout shade suction-cups to the window, blocks basically all light and heat, and cuts to size with scissors — no drill, no landlord. A piece cut for the back car window doubled the 11 a.m. car nap — previously a sweaty 20-minute disaster. The nursery one is still up nine months later.

Muslin swaddles — the utility fabric

Muslin swaddles did everything. A light swaddle on warm nights, a nursing cover that didn't suffocate anyone, a shade cloth draped over the stroller. The aden + anais 4-pack is about $25 and the cotton gets softer with every wash. Twenty-five dollars for the most useful fabric in human history.

The rules that kept us all safe

Errands before 10 a.m. or after 6 p.m. Touch the back of the neck: clammy means too hot; hot and dry means he's sweating inefficiently and you need to get inside. Never leave him in the car, even for a minute — put your phone in the back seat. And drink your own water, because a dehydrated mom is a useless mom. August babies are tough because their parents have to be. Keep a fan, a hat, and a cold towel handy.
